Linear Regression is a statistical model used to relate one or more continuous independent variables (or predictors) $X_1...X_p$ to a dependent variable $y$. The relationship between the variables is assumed to be linear. Linear regression is an example of a [[Generalized Linear Model]] where the response is assumed to follow a [[Normal]] distribution.

The general model is defined as:

$$y_i = \beta_0 + \beta_1X_{i1}+\beta_2X_{i2} + ... + \beta_pX_{ip} + \varepsilon_i$$
The parameters $\beta$ are interpreted as:
* $\beta_0$: The intercept term, representing the response if all predictors are 0
* $\beta_i$: The partial slopes, representing the change in the response variable for a unit change in $X_i$, keeping all other predictors equal

> [!example]- Model Example
> Given the linear regression model:
> $$\hat y = 10+2.5X_{1}+1.2X_{2} + \varepsilon_i$$
> The interpretations of the coefficients are:
> * $\beta_0 = 10$ so the expected response when all predictors are 0 is 10
> * $\beta_1 = 2.5$ so a unit change in $X_1$ will change $\hat y$ by 2.5
> * $\beta_2 = 1.2$ so a unit change in $X_2$ will change $\hat y$ by 1.2

## Matrix Representation

To accommodate multiple predictors efficiently, the model is expressed in matrix notation:

$$y=X\beta + \varepsilon$$

The matrices and vectors are defined as follows:

$$
X= \begin{pmatrix}
 1 & x_{11} & ... & x_{1p} \\
 1 & x_{21} & ... & x_{2p}  \\
 \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots  \\
 1 & x_{n1} & ... & x_{np} \\
\end{pmatrix}, \quad
y = \begin{pmatrix} y_1 \\ y_2 \\ \vdots \\ y_n \end{pmatrix}, \quad
\beta = \begin{pmatrix} \beta_0 \\ \beta_1 \\ \vdots \\ \beta_p \end{pmatrix}, \quad
\varepsilon = \begin{pmatrix} \varepsilon_1 \\ \varepsilon_2 \\ \vdots \\ \varepsilon_n \end{pmatrix}
$$

Where:
* $X$ is the $(n\times (p+1))$ **Design Matrix** containing $n$ data points and $p$ predictors. First column is all ones for bias term $\beta_0$
* $y$ is the $(n\times 1)$ vector of observations for the dependent variable.
* $\beta$ is the $((p+1)\times 1)$ vector of regression coefficients (parameters) to be estimated, including the intercept $\beta_0$.
* $\varepsilon$ is the $(n\times 1)$ vector of error terms (residuals).

## Estimation
The coefficients $\beta$ are unknown and must be estimated from the data.
* **Standard Estimation:** The most common method for estimating $\beta$ is [[Ordinary Least Squares (OLS)]], which minimizes the sum of squared residuals.
	* $\hat{\beta} = (X^T X)^{-1} X^T y$
* **Alternative Estimation:** Under the assumption of normally distributed errors, [[Maximum Likelihood Estimation]] can also be used, which yields the same results as OLS for linear regression.

Once the coefficients are estimated (denoted as $\hat{\beta}$), the predicted values $\hat{y}$ are generated by the linear combination of the design matrix and the estimated coefficients:

$$\hat{y} = X\hat{\beta}$$

## Model Diagnostics & Inference
* **Assumptions:** For the estimates to be reliable, specific conditions must be met. These are detailed in [[Linear Regression Assumptions]].
* **Hypothesis Testing:** To determine if the predictors are statistically significant, T-tests (for individual coefficients) and F-tests (for the overall model) are utilized. See [[Linear Regression Inference]].
* **Goodness of Fit:** The quality of the model is evaluated using metrics such as $R^2$ and the Residual Sum of Squares. See [[Goodness of Fit]].
* **Model Selection:** When choosing between multiple models, criteria such as [[AIC & BIC]] are employed to balance goodness of fit with model complexity.

## Special Case: Simple Linear Regression
The simplest form of linear regression involves a single predictor ($p=1$). The model is expressed as:
$$y_i = \beta_0 + \beta_1X_i + \epsilon_i$$

This represents a line of best fit defined by a slope ($\beta_1$) and a y-intercept ($\beta_0$). In this specific case, the OLS estimators for $\beta_0$ and $\beta_1$ are calculated as:

$$\hat \beta_1 = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^n(x_i-\bar x)(y_i-\bar y)}{\sum_{i=1}^n(x_i-\bar x)^2},\quad \hat \beta_0=\bar y - \hat \beta_1 \bar x$$

Where $\bar x$ and $\bar y$ represent the sample means of the predictor and target variables, respectively.

### Example
> [!example]- Simple Linear Regression Calculation
> A regression line is calculated and plotted for the following data:
> $x = [3, 3, 4, 6, 7, 9]$
> $y = [1, 4, 6, 4, 8, 7]$
> 
> **Step 1: Calculate Means**
> The sample means are determined:
> $\bar x = 5.333$
> $\bar y = 5$
> 
> **Step 2: Calculate Coefficients**
> The slope ($\hat \beta_1$) and intercept ($\hat \beta_0$) are derived:
> $$\hat \beta_1 = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^n(x_i-\bar x)(y_i-\bar y)}{\sum_{i=1}^n(x_i-\bar x)^2} = \frac{(3-5.333)(1-5)+...+(9-5.333)(7-5)}{(3-5.333)^2 +... + (9-5.333)^2} = 0.75$$
> $$\hat \beta_0=\bar y - \hat \beta_1 \bar x = 5 - (0.75)(5.333) = 1$$
> 
> **Result**
> The final estimator for the target variable is established as:
> $$\hat y = 1 + 0.75x$$
