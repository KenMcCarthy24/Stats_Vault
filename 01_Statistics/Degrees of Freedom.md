The number of independent values or quantities which can be assigned to a statistical distribution is referred to as the **Degrees of Freedom (DoF)**. It is often described as the number of values in the final calculation of a statistic that are free to vary.

Conceptually, DoF can be understood as the amount of information provided by the data that can be "spent" to estimate the parameters of the population.

## Constraints
The calculation of degrees of freedom is fundamentally linked to the number of constraints imposed on the data.
* If a set of $n$ data points is collected, there are initially $n$ degrees of freedom.
* When a parameter (such as the mean) is estimated from that data, a constraint is introduced. For each parameter estimated, one degree of freedom is lost.
* The remaining degrees of freedom are calculated as the sample size minus the number of parameters estimated.

> **Example:** If the sum of 3 numbers ($a, b, c$) is required to be 10, the first two numbers ($a$ and $b$) can be chosen freely. However, once $a$ and $b$ are determined, $c$ is fixed. Thus, there are 2 degrees of freedom.

# Common Applications and Formulas

### Sample Variance
When the [[Sample Variance and Standard Deviation|unbiased sample variance]] ($s^2$) is calculated, the [[Sample Mean]] ($\bar{x}$) must first be computed. Because the sum of deviations from the mean is always zero ($\sum(x - \bar{x}) = 0$), the last deviation is determined by the others.

* **Constraint:** 1 (The sample mean)
* **Formula:** $df = n - 1$

### Distribution Parameter
Degrees of freedom is sometimes used as a parameter for a probability distribution when a number of independent random variables are being combined to generate that distribution.

* [[Chi-Squared]]
	* The Chi-Squared distribution is the sum of squares of $n$ standard [[Normal]] random variables. $\sum_{i=1}^{n} Z_i^2 \sim \chi^2(n)$ where $Z \sim N(0, 1)$
	* The parameter is called "degrees of freedom" because the sum is composed of exactly $n$ sources of variation, the random variables $Z_i$
* [[Student-t]]
    * A Student's t-distribution is generated by the ratio of a standard Normal random variable to the square root of a Chi-Squared random variable (divided by its degrees of freedom). $T = \frac{Z}{\sqrt{U/n}}$ where $Z \sim N(0,1)$ and $U \sim \chi^2(n)$
    * The degrees of freedom parameter $n$ corresponds to the degrees of freedom of the Chi-squared variable in the denominator.
    * As the degrees of freedom increase ($n \to \infty$), the t-distribution converges to the standard Normal distribution.

* [[F-distribution]]
    * An F-distribution is generated by taking the ratio of two independent Chi-square variables (each divided by their respective degrees of freedom).
    * The F distribution is parameterized by the numerator degrees of freedom $d_1$ and the denominator degrees of freedom $d2$.
    * These are the number of independent square normal random variables summed to create the Chi-square in the numerator and denominator of the ratio. 


### Simple Linear Regression
In a  [[Linear Regression]] model ($\hat{y} = \beta_0 + \beta_1x_1 + ... +\beta_px_p$), $p+1$ parameters are estimated: the intercept ($\beta_0$) and the coefficients ($\beta_1,\beta_2, ...)$.

* **Constraint:** $p+1$
* **Residual Degrees of Freedom:** $df = n - (p+1)$

## Hypothesis Testing
Degrees of freedom are utilized to select the specific probability distribution required for [[Hypothesis Test|hypothesis testing]] (e.g., the specific t-distribution or Chi-square distribution).

The critical value required to reject a null hypothesis is dependent on the degrees of freedom; lower $dof$ usually results in higher critical values (thicker tails in the distribution).
