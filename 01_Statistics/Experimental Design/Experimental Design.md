
Experimental design is the branch of statistics used to establish **causality** (cause and effect) rather than just observing correlations (co-occurrence).

There are two major categories of scientific studies, experimental design is primarily concerned with the second:
1. **Observational Studies**: Studies in which the researcher does not interact with a process at all, merely observing pre-existing conditions. Used to identify correlations between variables
2. **Experimental Studies**: Studies in which the researcher actively interacts with the process in a designed and controlled manner in order to identify causation between variables. 

---

## Terminology
* **Experimental Units:** The smallest entities to which treatments are applied (e.g., people, plants, test tubes).
* **Factor:** The explanatory variable(s) being studied (e.g., "Dosage").
* **Levels:** The specific values or categories of a factor (e.g., 5mg, 10mg, 15mg).
* **Treatment:** A specific condition applied to the units. If there is only one factor, the treatment and level are the same. If there are multiple factors, a treatment is a combination of levels.
* **Response Variable:** The outcome being measured to see the effect of the treatment.

---
## Three Pillars of Experimental Design
To produce valid, unbiased results, an experiment must include:

1.  **Randomization:** Using chance to assign units to treatments. This helps balance out variables you aren't measuring and reduces bias.
2.  **Replication:** Applying the treatment to many units to reduce the impact of "fluke" results or chance variation.
3.  **Control:** Keeping other variables constant (or using a control group) to ensure the only thing causing a change in the response is the treatment itself.

---

> [!Example]- Basic Example Experiment
> Consider the following experiment:
> 
> A tech company is developing a new thermal paste to keep laptop processors cool. They want to compare their new formula (**Formula B**) against the current standard (**Formula A**) and a baseline (**No Paste**)
> 
> They also suspect that the type of fan setting used (**Silent Mode** vs **Performance Mode**) might change how well the paste works. They have 60 otherwise identical laptop units available for testing. After running a standardized processing heavy task for one hour, they measure the internal temperature of each CPU in degrees Celsius.
> 
> Below is a summary of the various components of this experiment:
> * **Experimental Units**: The 60 laptops
> * **Factors**: There are two factors in this experiment
> 	1. Thermal Paste Type
> 	2. Fan Setting
> * **Levels**: Each factor has different levels:
> 	* Thermal Paste Type: (Formula A, Formula B, No Paste)
> 	* Fan Setting: (Silent Mode, Performance Mode)
> * **Treatments**: The various possible combinations of the levels for the two factors. E.g. (Formula A, Silent Mode) or (No Paste, Performance Mode)
> * **Response Variable**: The internal temperature of the CPU in degrees Celsius
